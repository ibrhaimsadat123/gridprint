Every plug in Pakistan hums the same changing note.

GridPrint writes that note down every second. A video or voice note that caught the hum can be checked against it: when it was really recorded, and whether anything was cut out.

The grid never sits still.

Power stations and the whole country's demand never balance exactly, so the frequency drifts around 50 Hz, by a few thousandths every second. That wandering line never repeats.

2 of 4

A station writes it down.

A GridPrint station on a wall socket in Islamabad times every cycle of the mains and logs the frequency once a second. When the mains goes off and an inverter takes over, it notices that too.

3 of 4

Recordings overhear it.

Lights, fans and chargers leak the same hum into any microphone in the room. It is faint, but the wiggles are all there, carried at 100, 200 and 300 Hz.

Built by two roommates, one socket at a time.

GridPrint started in a NUST hostel room in Islamabad, where Ibrahim Sadat (maths) and Abdullah Habib (mechanical engineering) asked whether the electricity itself could act as a clock that nobody can fake. The station is an Arduino, an optocoupler and a small transformer. The explorer, the inverter detection and the matching are open source.
